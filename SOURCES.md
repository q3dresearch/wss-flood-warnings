## Each source in full

### `ea.flood.warnings`

**Environment Agency** (first party) · hourly · active

Live UK flood alerts and warnings from the Environment Agency, captured hourly because the publisher deletes them after about a day.
WHY THIS IS HOURLY AND ALMOST NOTHING ELSE IS. The fleet's floor is weekly and the engine enforced it until 2026-09-23. It was relaxed for this source and the reasoning is worth keeping, because it is the test any future hourly source has to pass: the publisher documents a deletion window of ~24 hours, which is shorter than the sampling interval by a factor of seven. A weekly reading of a register that empties daily is not a coarse sample, it is a lottery -- it would catch roughly one flood event in seven and report success on the other six. Compare wss-gho or wss-carbon-registry, where a state persists for months and weekly buys nothing over monthly.
THE CENTRAL QUESTION, WHICH NOBODY CAN CURRENTLY ANSWER. This register is unusual in having BOTH a terminal status and a deletion. severityLevel 4 ("Warning no longer in force") is set first; the row is removed some time after. How long is that second window? The publisher does not say, and nothing in the archive can tell you, because answering it needs consecutive observations closer together than the window itself. Hourly capture measures it directly: the number of consecutive captures in which a given floodAreaID is present at severityLevel 4 IS the answer. That is a fact about how a public warning system retires its records, and it is not recoverable later.
THE ARCHIVE DOES NOT COVER THIS, AND HERE IS THE NUMBER. The Internet Archive holds 57 distinct captures of /flood-monitoring/id/floods across 2017-2026 -- one per 61 days against a 24-hour turnover -- distributed 2017:11, 2018:18, 2019:13, 2020:1, 2021:3, 2024:2, 2025:6, 2026:3. There are three captures in the whole of 2026. The sibling ?min-severity=3 view has 15. So essentially every flood event since 2017 has already been lost, and the ones the Archive did catch are luck rather than coverage. This is the thinnest archive coverage measured against any source in this fleet; for contrast, Apple's App Store Review Guidelines carry ~1,950.
WHAT A BUSY DAY LOOKS LIKE. The single largest memento, 2020-02-17 (Storm Dennis), is 1,096,729 bytes and 760 items: 6 Severe Flood Warnings, 193 Flood Warnings, 248 Flood Alerts and 313 already marked "Warning no longer in force". That last number is the point -- more than 40% of a storm-day payload is the terminal state, and all of it is deleted within about a day.
A QUIET HOUR IS 703 BYTES AND items: [], AND THAT IS REAL DATA. Checked 2026-09-23: zero active warnings nationally. Two fetches three seconds apart were BYTE-IDENTICAL, so the payload carries no nonce, no served-at stamp and no rotating id -- dedupe works on this source with no dedupe_ignore at all, and an unchanged hour costs nothing but a manifest line. That is what makes hourly affordable. It also means an empty response must never be treated as a failed fetch: silence here is a measurement that no part of England is flooding, which is most hours of most years.
WHICH IS WHY max_shrink_pct IS 100. A storm payload is 1.1 MB and the hour after the last warning is withdrawn it is 703 bytes -- a 99.94% shrink that is completely correct and happens after every flood event. Any shrink gate tight enough to be meaningful would quarantine precisely the captures that record a flood ENDING, which is the signal. The floor that actually protects this source is min_bytes plus must_contain: an error page or a truncated response fails those, and a real empty register passes them.
PARSED BY parsers/floodwarning_v1.py, keyed on floodAreaID -- the identifier that resolves to a published flood-area polygon and is therefore what anything outside this register can join on. The numeric warning id from @id is carried as a metric instead, so a SECOND flood at the same area is visible as a changed warning_id rather than being silently merged into the first. All three candidate keys were checked for uniqueness on the 760-item storm payload: floodAreaID, @id and (floodAreaID, timeRaised) are each 760 distinct over 760 rows.

## Reusing this data

The captures in this repository are bytes as the publisher served them. Each
source's own licence or terms of service govern reuse — they are listed per
source above and they are not all the same. This repository's code is separate
from the data it collects; check the source before redistributing.
